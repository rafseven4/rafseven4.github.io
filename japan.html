First Impressions

A country that rewards curiosity

Touching down in Tokyo after 14 hours in the air, you feel the shift immediately. The station signs are bilingual. The trains are silent and on time. The vending machines work without question. Everything is efficient, polite, and somehow still warm.

Two weeks later, you understand why Japan sits in the global imagination the way it does. It's not just beautiful. It's thoughtful.

Osaka

The kitchen of Japan · Street food · Energy

Osaka · Dotonbori

This city feeds you.

Osaka doesn't whisper. It shouts. Neon signs, food stall after food stall, the smell of takoyaki frying and the sound of the crowds — it's sensory overload in the best possible way.

We spent an entire evening moving through Dotonbori, trying everything. Okonomiyaki. Yakitori. Ramen that made you forget why you ever ate ramen anywhere else. Osaka is unapologetically about pleasure — food, energy, celebration.

"Eat until you're happy, not until you're full."

Nara · Sacred Park

The deer
own this place

Nobody warned us just how assertive the deer of Nara are. They bow for shika senbei crackers — actual bowing — and then immediately headbutt you when they want more. Sacred but absolutely not tame.

We wandered through the park for hours. The Great Buddha of Tōdai-ji is genuinely staggering in scale — you understand why people crossed oceans to see it. A day trip from Kyoto that felt like a week.

Fuji-san & Tokyo

The sacred mountain · The electric megalopolis

Yamanashi · Mount Fuji

The mountain
needs no filter

We made the day trip to Lake Kawaguchi for the classic Fuji view — perfect snow cap, clear April sky, cherry blossom still clinging to the trees along the shore. The famous convenience store view. All of it.

Standing there with Fuji filling the horizon, you understand why it has been painted, worshipped, and climbed for thousands of years. It's just bigger than everything else. More present. The Japanese call it Fuji-san — not Mount Fuji — and that -san suffix, used for respected elders, feels exactly right.

"Some things earn their legend."
Tokyo · Final Chapter

Tokyo hits
like a wave

After the quietude of Kyoto and Nara, Tokyo is a deliberate shock to the system. Shinjuku at midnight. Shibuya crossing with its organized human chaos. Akihabara's sensory overload. Yanaka's old-world calm just a subway stop away.

Tokyo is a city that somehow functions at a scale that shouldn't work — 14 million people, all of them seemingly on time, all of them impossibly polite. We ate conveyor-belt sushi, visited the teamLab digital art museum, and got genuinely lost in Shimokitazawa's vintage shops.

If Osaka fed us and Kyoto quieted us and Nara charmed us, Tokyo finished us off — in the best possible way.

Japan doesn't just impress you — it recalibrates you.

Two weeks was not enough. It never is with Japan. You leave with a list that's longer than the one you arrived with — more places to return to, more foods to try, more neighbourhoods to wander through.

The politeness, the obsessive care in everything — the perfectly wrapped convenience store onigiri, the train that apologised for being thirty seconds late, the ryokan owner who left a hand-written note explaining every detail of breakfast — all of it adds up to something hard to name but easy to feel.

We'll be back. That's not a question.
